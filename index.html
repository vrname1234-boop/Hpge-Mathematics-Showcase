AN EXPLORATION IN NUMBER THEORY

6 = 1 + 2 + 3

Add up everything that divides 6 evenly and you get 6 back. It sounds simple, but only 52 numbers are known to pull it off. This site is about why that is so rare, and why mathematicians have been hooked on it for over two thousand years.

01

THE DEFINITION

A perfect number equals the sum of the numbers that divide it evenly, not counting itself.

Take 6. The numbers that divide it evenly are 1, 2 and 3. Add them up and you get 6 back. The number rebuilds itself from its own parts.

That almost never happens. Mathematicians write the idea like this:

σ(n) = 2n

σ is the Greek letter sigma. Here it means "add up every divisor of n, including n itself". If the total lands exactly on double the number, the number is perfect.

THE BROKEN GUESS

Look at the first four perfect numbers. They have 1, 2, 3 and 4 digits. A Greek writer named Nicomachus declared there was exactly one perfect number of each size, marching neatly upward.

Then the fifth one turned up with 8 digits. His pattern collapsed, and it took over a thousand years of work just to find that fifth number at all.

THE FORMULA

P = 2p−1 × (2p − 1)

Here is how to read it. Pick a small prime p. Work out 2p − 1. If that answer is also prime, it is called a Mersenne prime, and multiplying it by 2p−1 gives you a perfect number. Euler proved there is no other way to make an even one.

SIGMA EXPLORER

σ(n)

That symbol is the Greek letter sigma. In maths it means "add up all the divisors of n, including n itself". Try it on 6: the divisors are 1, 2, 3 and 6, so σ(6) = 12. That is exactly double 6, which is why 6 is perfect.

Compare σ(n) to double the number, and every integer lands in one of three classes:

σ < 2nDEFICIENTThe divisors add up to less than double. Most numbers live here.
σ = 2nPERFECTThe divisors add up to exactly double. Almost never happens.
σ > 2nABUNDANTThe divisors add up to more than double.

THE FAMILY

Here is a game mathematicians play. Take a number, add up its divisors (not counting itself), and let the answer become the next number. Repeat. That chain is called an aliquot sequence. Some chains stop at 1. Some loop forever. And the chain starting at 276 is still going after a century of trying. Nobody knows if it ever ends.

AMICABLE NUMBERS

Sometimes a chain does not stop at one number. It bounces between two forever.

220↔284

The divisors of 220 add up to 284. The divisors of 284 add up to 220. Mathematicians call them amicable numbers. Type 220 into the walker and watch it happen.

The next pair, 1184 and 1210, was found in 1860 by a sixteen-year-old Italian schoolboy named Nicolò Paganini.

05 · THE OPEN PROBLEM

DOES AN ODD PERFECT NUMBER EXIST?

Every perfect number ever found is even. All 52 of them. Does an odd one exist? Nobody knows.

It is one of the oldest unanswered questions in mathematics, standing for over two thousand years. Nobody has found an odd perfect number, and nobody has proved one cannot exist. The search has not been useless though. Mathematicians have worked out exactly what such a number would have to look like:

  • It would have to be bigger than 101500. That is a number with 1,501 digits, far beyond anything physically meaningful.
  • It would need at least 101 prime factors, counting repeats.
  • Its largest prime factor would have to be bigger than 100 million.
  • It cannot be divisible by 105.

A number that big and that specific might not exist at all. But nobody has proved it either way. It is still out there, or it isn't.

That is why perfect numbers matter.

The rule for even ones was finished over 2,300 years ago. The odd version sits right next to it, still open. One side solved, one side a mystery.

From Euclid's proof to a volunteer's laptop in 2024, this is one of the longest searches in mathematics.
